A coin, three ways

A coin has some bias — a probability θ of landing heads — and we do not know what it is. Toss it, write down what happened, and then reason about it three times over: with a classical test, with maximum likelihood, and with Bayes. The tosses below belong to all three tabs, so what changes between them is the method and nothing else.

Classical test Maximum likelihood Bayes Generator

Put a hypothesis about the coin on trial. The hypothesis has to say how often the coin lands heads — its P(heads): a fair coin is 0.5, a two-headed one is 1. If the coin really did land heads that often, how surprising would the tosses we have be? That is the p-value, and if it falls below α the hypothesis is rejected.

Take the bias that makes these tosses likeliest, which for a coin is the share of heads. The line across the curve is 1.92 log-units below its peak, and everything above that line is the likelihood-ratio interval. That share of heads is the best estimate of P(heads), written θ̂, and the Wald interval — θ̂ ± 1.96 √(θ̂(1 − θ̂)/n) — is drawn beside it.
